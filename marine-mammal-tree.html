Three lineages, one way of life

Back to the sea

Bats are a clade: every one of them descends from a single flying ancestor. “Marine mammal” is not. It is a job description that three unrelated groups of land mammals filled independently — whales from hoofed stock, sirenians from the elephant side of the tree, seals from the carnivorans — and the resemblance between them is something water imposed, not something they inherited.

I

The ladder of ranks

Background: what a rank actually is, and where four marine mammals land when you file them from kingdom down to species.

A rank is just a level of nesting. Each step down describes a smaller, more closely related group, and every group at every level is contained entirely inside the one above it. Family sits in the middle: broad enough to share an obvious body plan and way of life, narrow enough that its members are unmistakably kin.

Follow any of the four below and watch where it splits from the others. They share Kingdom, Phylum and Class — and then nothing. There is no rung at which “marine mammal” appears, because the group was never one thing to begin with. Note where the whales sit: inside Artiodactyla, the even-toed hoofed mammals, alongside the hippos that are their closest living relatives.

    II

    The flat atlas, with filters

    The same content as the tree, laid out flat and filterable by ocean, diet, where it rests, biosonar or regional checklist. Filtering here also dims the tree above.

    All 18 families opened up, grouped by lineage: subfamilies, tribes where they are used, and every genus, with the number of species in each. Genera marked with a dot open one level further, to the species themselves.

    Every genus lists every one of its species. With 144 species in total there is no need for the labelled-selection compromise a group of 1,500-plus would force — the largest genus here, Mesoplodon, has 16.

    The filters below work on genera, and the cladogram above dims to match. Ocean, diet and haul-out are labelled at genus level, which is blunt: a wide-ranging genus will always have exceptions to whatever label it carries. Biosonar is labelled at family level, and only as a yes or no — see the note in the footer on why there are no call figures here. The two regional checklists are curated species lists, not derived from the range data.

    III

    Where to look next

    Each species above carries links to search results, not to individual pages — a search box always resolves, whereas a guessed page address often does not. Coverage is good for the large whales, the temperate dolphins and the northern seals, and thin for beaked whales, which is a fair reflection of how little anyone has seen of them.

    Society for Marine Mammalogy

    Its Committee on Taxonomy maintains the standing list of marine mammal species and subspecies, revised annually. This is the specialist authority for the group, and the reference to check this page against — it will drift ahead of what MDD has folded in.

    Watkins Marine Mammal Sound Database

    Woods Hole's archive of roughly 70 years of recordings, covering about 60 species — the closest thing this group has to a single open call library. Start here for what any of these animals actually sound like.

    iNaturalist

    Photographs of live animals, community-identified. Strong for coastal species and whale-watching routes, sparse for anything that lives past the shelf edge. This is what the "photos" link searches.

    GBIF

    Aggregated occurrence records from museums, surveys and strandings worldwide. Good for answering "where has this actually been recorded", and the source of the country supplement merged into the map on this page.

    IUCN Red List

    Conservation status, threats and range maps, written by specialist assessors. The place to check before trusting any distribution statement here — and the source for the status shown on every species record.

    Mammal Diversity Database

    The American Society of Mammalogists' running list of every mammal species, updated a few times a year and downloadable as CSV. Its taxonomy table is the source for every count, rank and country on this page. Version 2.5 places all 102 cetaceans inside Artiodactyla, and unlike its bat coverage it fills in subfamily and tribe for these families itself.

    OBIS

    The Ocean Biodiversity Information System — the marine counterpart to GBIF, pooling survey and tracking data. Better than any country list for animals whose real range is a current, a shelf edge or a migration corridor rather than a coastline.

    Natural Earth

    Public-domain map data. The range map is drawn from Natural Earth's country outlines with small islands marked as dots, and — unlike the bat page's map — it keeps Antarctica, which 28 of these species need. The countries themselves come from MDD and GBIF, not from Natural Earth: the map only draws what the checklist already says.

    How firm is any of this? The three lineages are not in doubt, and neither is the awkward fact underneath them: they are not each other's closest relatives. Whales sit inside Artiodactyla next to hippos, sirenians belong with elephants and hyraxes, pinnipeds with bears and weasels. Below that it softens. Whether the pinnipeds entered the water once or twice was argued for a century — one origin is now the settled answer — and the split drawn here between Otarioidea and Phocoidea is a convenience, not a consensus. Inside Delphinidae the arrangement is frankly unstable, which is why MDD leaves Orcinus, Lagenorhynchus and Leucopleurus in incertae sedis rather than forcing them into a subfamily.

    Where the numbers come from. Every count here is taken from the Mammal Diversity Database v2.5: 144 living and recently extinct species, 67 genera, 18 families. Unlike the bat page, no rank on this page is supplied by hand — MDD's own table carries subfamily and tribe for these families, so the atlas groupings, the cladogram and the ladder are all read straight out of it. The lineage totals are Cetacea 102, Pinnipedia 37, Sirenia 5.

    Why there are no call figures. The toothed whales are among the most accomplished echolocators alive, and the sperm whale's click is the loudest sound any animal makes. None of that is quantified here. The bat page carries a hand-compiled table of call type, duty cycle and peak frequency per family; no equivalent reference was compiled for these animals, and inventing one would have produced numbers that look citable and are not. The filter therefore answers only "does this family hunt with biosonar", which is a fact rather than a measurement. Pinnipeds answer no: seals track prey by hearing, sight, and in several species by reading the hydrodynamic wake a fish leaves behind with their whiskers, which is not sonar.

    The numbers move, and three have stopped. Species counts here are rising for the same reason they are everywhere — genetics splitting one widespread species into several. MDD v2.5 breaks the killer whale into three, cuts Lagenorhynchus down to a single species with the rest moved to Leucopleurus and Aethalodelphis, and folds the grey whale into Balaenopteridae. Three species in this list are already gone: Steller's sea cow, hunted out within 27 years of being described; the Caribbean monk seal; and the Japanese sea lion. The baiji is still listed as living, which is a formality — a full survey of the Yangtze in 2006 found none.
