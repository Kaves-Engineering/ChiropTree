I

The ladder of ranks

A rank is just a level of nesting. Each step down describes a smaller, more closely related group, and every group at every level is contained entirely inside the one above it. Family sits in the middle: broad enough to share an obvious body plan and way of life, narrow enough that its members are unmistakably kin.

Pick a bat and watch the ladder narrow. The bar under each rung shows how many bat species fall inside that group.

    II

    The tree of families

    Genetic evidence broke the old split between big fruit bats and small echolocating bats. Flying foxes turned out to be nested beside horseshoe bats, not opposite all the rest — so the two suborders below are Yinpterochiroptera and Yangochiroptera. Superfamilies group the families inside each.

    III

    Inside every family

    All 21 families opened up: subfamilies, tribes where they are used, and every genus, with the number of species in each. Genera marked with a dot open one level further, to the species themselves.

    Where a genus is small, every species is listed. Where it is large — Myotis has 143 — you get a labelled selection rather than a wall of Latin, and the panel says so. Each species carries an echolocation line: a measured description where one exists, otherwise the family profile, shown in grey italics so you can tell the two apart.

    The filters below work on genera, and the cladogram above dims to match. Diet, roost and range are labelled at genus level, which is blunt: a genus of 130 species will always have exceptions to whatever label it carries. Echolocation is labelled at family level. The two regional checklists are curated species lists, not derived from the range data.

    IV

    Where to look next

    Each species above carries three links. They point at search results, not at individual pages — I can't verify 623 separate URLs, and a search box always resolves whereas a guessed page address often doesn't. Expect good coverage for European, North American and Australian bats and thin coverage for tropical ones.

    ChiroVox

    The largest open-access bat call library, run by the Hungarian Natural History Museum and SEABCRU. Around 4,000 recordings from close to 200 species, most from bats caught and identified in the hand, so the identifications are trustworthy. Best single source for echolocation.

    xeno-canto

    Long-established wildlife sound archive that added bats in 2022. Every recording comes with a spectrogram and a map, all under Creative Commons licences. This is what the "calls" link searches.

    iNaturalist

    Photographs of live animals, community-identified. Strong where people go bat-watching, sparse elsewhere. This is what the "photos" link searches.

    GBIF

    Aggregated occurrence records from museums and surveys worldwide, with distribution maps and specimen media. Good for answering "where has this actually been found".

    IUCN Red List

    Conservation status, threats and range maps, written by specialist assessors. The place to check before trusting any distribution statement on this page.

    Mammal Diversity Database

    The American Society of Mammalogists' running list of every mammal species, updated a few times a year and downloadable as CSV. Its taxonomy table is the source for every count on this page. Version 2.5, released 28 July 2026, recognises 21 bat families, 238 genera and 1,506 living species, plus 8 gone extinct in the last 500 years. Archived releases carry a DOI, so a count can be cited to a specific version.

    Bat Species of the World

    Simmons and Cirranello's taxonomic database — the authority the classification on this page follows. Updated twice a year, so it will drift ahead of what you see here.

    How firm is any of this? The 21 families and the two suborders are well supported. Below that it gets softer. The branching order inside superfamilies is not settled — Myzopodidae and Miniopteridae in particular move between studies. Subfamilies and tribes are conventions that different authorities draw differently: the arrangement here follows Simmons and Cirranello's Bat Species of the World, with the Phyllostomidae subfamilies of Baker and colleagues (2016). Under other schemes you will see Antrozoinae and Nyctophilinae raised to subfamilies, Chaerephon kept apart from Mops, and Lasiurus split into three.

    Where the numbers come from. Every genus and species count on this page is taken from the Mammal Diversity Database v2.5 (28 July 2026), which the ASM harmonises with batnames.org through the IUCN Bat Specialist Group. Totals reconcile exactly: 238 genera and 1,506 living species across 21 families. Subfamily and tribe placements are mine, following Simmons and Cirranello with the Phyllostomidae arrangement of Baker and colleagues (2016), since MDD's table does not display those ranks.

    Recent changes worth knowing. MDD v2.5 differs sharply from most older references. Eptesicus has been broken up and now holds only two species, with the serotine and northern bat moved to Cnephaeus and the Neotropical species to Neoeptesicus — which is why the Danish and British checklists here read Cnephaeus serotinus. Most Asian pipistrelles have gone to Alionoctula, leaving Pipistrellus with 16 species. Chaerephon is inside Mops, Micropteropus inside Epomophorus, Thainycteris inside Arielulus, and Aeorestes and Dasypterus back inside Lasiurus. The 1,500th bat species, Pipistrellus etula, was recognised in v2.3 in 2025.

    The numbers move. Species counts are approximate and rising — MDD v1.0 listed about 1,300 bat species in 2018 and v2.5 lists 1,506 now. Very little of that is genuinely new discovery in the field; most of it is genetic work finding that one widespread species is really several that look alike. Genus totals shift the same way, in both directions, as lineages are split or merged. Treat every figure here as "about", not "exactly", and expect the vesper bats, horseshoe bats and bent-winged bats to keep changing fastest.
